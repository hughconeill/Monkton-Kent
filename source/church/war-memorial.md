## 1914 – 1918

### Private Charles Andrews (1879–1918), aged 39

Born in Minster, 7th of 9 children. A lodger in Monkton from the 1901 census. He married Clara and they lived at Sheriffs Court Lane. Enlisted in 1914 with 7th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment). Spent most of the War in the Flanders trenches, especially the Somme. He was engaged in the Battles of the Selle and the Sambre Canal. He died 4 days after their withdrawal from fighting, presumably wounded. His name is on a memorial to the missing at Arras, Vis-en-Artois Memorial.

### Private John Carthew (1879–1915), aged 36

He was born in Monkton, the 6th child of Charles & Emma, and lived at Gore Street, then Solihull Cottages (near Sheriffs Court Lane). His father Charles was buried at Monkton on 22nd August 1906 aged 67 – his mother Emma was also buried there on 22nd June 1933 aged 92. He was a regular soldier and could have seen action before WW1. He enlisted again in Canterbury into the Buffs Regiment who went to France in January 1915, soon fighting the 2nd Battle of Ypres. Under intense fighting he did not survive and was killed in action on 3rd May 1915. His body was never recovered. His name is inscribed on the Menin Gate Memorial at Ypres in Belgium.

### Private Ernest Fitall (1887–1918), aged 31

He was born 9th October 1887 in Eastry. The family lived at “Monkton Cottage”, Monkton; his sisters were married at Monkton Church, and his father, Franklin, was buried in Monkton Churchyard on 15th October 1909 aged 46, unmarked. His mother, Fanny Fitall was buried on 21st December 1942 aged 76, unmarked but near her son Ernest. He enlisted in Dover on 23rd May 1915, and posted to the 3rd Battalion The Buffs which was a training outfit receiving recruits and sending them overseas. Then he was posted to the 6th Battalion fighting in France, especially on the Somme. On 7th October 1916 he was wounded and evacuated back to England for many months. Unable to return to the fighting, he was posted to the Labour Corps who worked to repair Britain's infrastructure during the War. But he became a victim of the influenza epidemic sweeping the country, dying in the Military Hospital, Canterbury on 31st October 1918. He was buried in Monkton Churchyard with full military honours by Revd Brackenbury.

**Official record:** [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/find-records/find-war-dead/casualty-details/365926/ernest-fitall/) –
The Buffs (East Kent Regiment), 3rd Bn., service no. 6453 (latterly 426th
Agricultural Company, Labour Corps). Son of Fanny Fitall of Monkton Street.
Buried in grave [#182](churchyard/numbered-stones.md), "South of Church
tower, near South boundary", which has a Commonwealth War Grave headstone
inscribed "R.I.P. THY WILL BE DONE."

### Private George Ernest Foad (c1889–1917), aged 27

The family lived in Monkton Street near “Parsonage Farm”. Prior to enlistment he worked as a colliery tip man, presumably at Chislet. He enlisted on 10th December 1915, posted to the 10th Service Battalion, destined to be sent to France in 2 months, but had to be sent back reporting sick and hospitalised with pleurisy. He was discharged, on pension on 13th July 1916. He was an Army pensioner from the Queen’s Royal West Surrey regiment. He died on 22nd April 1917 from TB at home in Gore Street. He was buried at St Nicholas at Wade with Monkton vicar Revd Brackenbury officiating.

### Private Cecil Frederick Garlinge (c1895–1917), aged 22

He was born in Chislet, Kent, and lived in Birchington. He enlisted with the 6th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment). They attacked the German line at Cambrai, France, on 20th November 1917 – the first action in which tanks were used in large numbers. The attack was a success, but unfortunately Cecil was among the dead and his body never found. His name is inscribed on Panel 3 of the Cambrai Memorial at Louverval, France.

### Private William James Garlinge (1885–1916), aged 31

He was the older brother of Cecil, born at Ash, and living in Birchington. He enlisted into the 8th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment) and was engaged from September 1915 in the Battle of Loos. During a successful special raid on the German trenches, he was wounded as his Regiment entered the Battle of the Somme. He died on 17th July 1916 and buried in the Bailleul Communal Cemetery Extension (between St Omer and Lille, south-west of Ypres). He was awarded the Military Medal, and Mentioned in Despatches.

### Private Albert William Hammond (d.1917)

The eldest of 4 children, born at Ash, and resident in Monkton. He enlisted in Margate with the 7th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment), going to France in July 1915. He died of wounds 23rd July 1917 in Niederwehren, Germany, as a POW, wounded after the British offensive at Arras, France on 3rd May 1917.

### Private Frederick George Hammond (d.1915), aged 17

He was the 3rd of four children, and younger brother to Albert. They both enlisted at the same time with the 7th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment). The Germans tunnelled under the Buffs front line and a mine was exploded on 1st September 1915. Frederick was injured and transferred to safety in Dernancourt Camp but died of his wounds on 29th September 1915. He is buried in Dernancourt Communal Cemetery.

### Private Frederick William Ireland (c1898–1917), aged 19

He was the 5th of 7 children, living at St Nicholas at Wade, Kent. He enlisted at Ramsgate, probably underage, and posted to the 1st Battalion South Staffordshire Regiment. He was killed in action on 26th October 1917 during the 3rd Battle of Ypres (Battle of Passchendaele). His body was never recovered and he is commemorated on the Tyne Cot Memorial to the Missing, near Ypres, the largest British cemetery on the Western Front.

### Stoker 1st Class Frederick Thomas Johnson (d.1915), aged 21

He lived in Acol. He was a Stoker 1st Class on H.M.S. “Vengeance” Royal Navy, and fighting in the Dardanelles, Turkey. During a heavy bombardment of the Turkish forts at the mouth of the Straits, it seems likely that he was in a landing party in a successful attempt to knock out 3 heavy guns, dying on 28th February 1915. His body was not recovered but he is commemorated on the Chatham Naval Memorial Panel 11.

### Private Charles Alfred Pritchard (c1883–1916), aged 33

He was born in Monkton, enlisted at Ramsgate and first serving in The Buffs. Later transferred to the 91st Battalion Machine Gun Corps at the end of 1915. He was killed in action during the Battle of the Somme on 15th July 1916, and buried in Caterpillar Valley Cemetery, France.

### Private Sidney (Sydney) Shonk (c1894–1916), aged 22

He was the 13th child of a farm labourer living variously in Monkton, his mother Sarah at #4 Solihull Cottages (also see “Oakley Cottage” Monkton). He enlisted into the 7th Battalion of The Buffs (East Kent Regiment) and was one of the 19,000 British soldiers killed on the first day of the Battle of the Somme, a day which saw 57,000 casualties. He is buried in Dantzig Alley Cemetery in France.

**Official record:** [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/find-records/find-war-dead/casualty-details/548339/sydney-shonk/) –
Private Sydney Shonk, The Buffs (East Kent Regiment), 7th Bn., died 1 July
1916 (the first day of the Battle of the Somme); buried at Dantzig Alley
British Cemetery, Mametz, France. Recorded as "son of the late Stephen and
Sarah Shonk" – almost certainly the Stephen and Sarah Shonk buried in the
[churchyard](churchyard/numbered-stones.md) alongside later generations of
the same family.

### Private Percy William Stevens (c1894–1918), aged 24

Born in Tooting, Middlesex but later his family moved into #5 Church Row Cottages, Monkton. He enlisted at Bedford initially into the Bedfordshire regiment, then transferred to the 1st Battalion Somerset Light Infantry. He died on the Western Front at the Battle of the Lys, wounded and then dying 6 days later on 21st April 1918. He was buried nearby in Lapugnoy Military Cemetery, France.

## 1939 – 1945

### Fusilier Arthur Nelson Dunk (1910–1944), aged 34

His family lived in Monkton and he enlisted in the 4th Battalion Royal Welsh Fusiliers. He was killed in action in France following the D-Day landings on 18th August 1944. He is buried in the War Cemetery at Banneville-La-Campagne, near Caen, France.

### Private Ernest Arthur Fagg (1925–1944), aged 19

He lived in Church Cottages, Monkton and worked at Monkton Court Farm with the turkeys. He enlisted into the Wiltshire regiment who were involved in the landing at Anzio, Italy and the advance on Rome. Ernest was killed in action on 22nd January 1944, and buried in the Minturno War Cemetery near Naples, Italy.

### Sergeant Norman Henry Hood DFM (d.1942), aged 22

His family lived in the Drove, Monkton, while Norman and his wife Queenie lived in Sheriffs Court Lane. He worked with the poultry at Monkton Court Farm. He enlisted with No. 35 Squadron, Royal Air Force Volunteer Reserve as a wireless operator flying in Halifax bombers. He was killed in action on 7th May 1942 when his aircraft was shot down in Belgium, returning from a raid on Stuttgart. He was awarded the Distinguished Flying Medal, and is buried in Neufchateau Communal Cemetery, Belgium.

### Sergeant Cyril Kenneth Smith (d.1942)

His family lived in Sheriffs Court Lane – father Percy ‘Dido’ Smith, his sister Queenie married Norman Hood. He served with the 4th Royal Tank Regiment, part of the 8th Army in North Africa. In May and June 1942 they were under fierce attack from Rommel’s drive towards Suez when he was killed in action 21st June 1942. He did not live to see Montgomery take over command and launch the Battle of El Alamein which turned the tide for the Allies and ended German ambitions in North Africa. He is buried in Knightsbridge War Cemetery in Libya.

### Sergeant Ronald Ernest Herbert Bushell (d.1944), aged 20

His family lived in Monkton, in “The Square” (between Shop & Post Office – W.S. Coles builders?). Whilst on leave from the Royal Air Force Volunteer Reserve he went to London to visit a relative, where he was killed by a V2 rocket bomb on 26th November 1944. He is buried in Ramsgate and St Lawrence Cemetery.

### Lance Bombardier Digby Alfred Fuller (d.1942), aged 22

Although originating from Woolwich, London, he lived at Solihull Cottages, Sheriffs Court Lane area. He enlisted with the 3rd Regiment Royal Horse Artillery and was fighting in the North Africa campaign. He was killed in action on 7th July 1942 near El Alamein, and prior to Montgomery’s command and the Battle of El Alamein in October that turned the tide in favour of the Allies. He is buried in the El Alamein Cemetery in Egypt.

The memorial closes with:

```{epigraph}
Grant them O Lord eternal rest and let light perpetual shine upon them.
```

## Names added later

Four further names appear separately on the memorial, associated with
the 1914–1918 list. Driver Jack Matthews was originally left off the
memorial; his name has since been added.

### Driver Jack Matthews (1889–1918), aged 29

He was born in Ramsgate on 25th November 1889, described as a gypsy and illiterate. He volunteered for the Army at Canterbury on 11th January 1917, having earlier served with 3rd Battalion The Buffs. He was appointed to the Army Service Corps as a driver. But by March 1917 there were concerns about his mental health and he was discharged from the Army as permanently unfit on 25th September 1917. He died 15 months later of flu and heart failure on 29th December 1918, and buried in Monkton churchyard by Revd Brackenbury (named “John” in the parish Burials Register!), but left off the Village War Memorial – now corrected. His family lived in a caravan on Monkton Marshes, on land at the bottom of the Drove.

**Official record:** [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/find-records/find-war-dead/casualty-details/365928/j-matthews/) –
Driver J. Matthews, Royal Army Service Corps, service no. 261564. Buried in
grave [#191](churchyard/numbered-stones.md), "South-West of Church, near
South boundary", which has a Commonwealth War Grave headstone.

### Lance Corporal William John Knight (d.1918), aged 34

He enlisted with the 6th Battalion The Buffs (East Kent Regiment), who were deployed near Albert, France. Their position was heavily shelled and William was probably wounded, dying the next day on 4th May 1918. His body is buried in the Bagneux British War Cemetery at Gezaincourt.

### Private Henry John Martin (d.1917), aged 28

He was born in Monkton, and married Clara Johnson from Stour Street, Canterbury. He enlisted with the 6th Battalion The Buffs (12th Division) and died during the Battle of Arras on 1st May 1917. It’s possible that whilst waiting for the battle he was killed by a sniper’s bullet or some accident, or even wounded previously but died of his injuries. He is buried in Faubourg d’Amiens Cemetery, Arras, France.

### Daniel Rogers

Born in Monkton. No other information.

## Other military graves in the churchyard

Two more servicemen of the First World War, not from Monkton, are buried
in the churchyard. Both were serving with the East Surrey Regiment,
which was training at Gore Street Camp.

The [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/visit-us/find-cemeteries-memorials/cemetery-details/39825/monkton-st-mary-magdalene-churchyard/)
records four First World War graves at St Mary Magdalene: these two men,
plus Private Ernest Fitall and Driver Jack Matthews (above). Each man's
official record is linked below his biography; see the
[churchyard survey](churchyard/survey-by-location.md) for the gravestones
themselves.

### Private James Booth Bradbury (d.1917), aged 19

Born in Saddleworth, Yorkshire; worked as a mill-grinder at Diggle Mill, Oldham, Lancashire. He enlisted and was in the 2nd/6th Battalion of the East Surrey Regiment. He was accidentally killed whilst training at Gore Street Camp. On Monkton Marshes, in a specially prepared bunker, live hand grenades were being used for the first time. His fellow private threw his grenade but it hit the top of the parapet and rolled back into the bunker. Despite the order to get clear, James unaccountably ran back into the bunker and was killed immediately. His funeral service was held in Monkton Church and he is buried in the churchyard ([#9](churchyard/numbered-stones.md)) – funds being provided by his village school and Boys Brigade in Diggle.

**Official record:** [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/find-records/find-war-dead/casualty-details/365925/james-booth-bradbury/) –
East Surrey Regiment, 2nd/6th Bn., service no. 242209. Son of Charles James
and Ann Bradbury, of Back-o'th-Lee, Diggle, Oldham. Buried in grave
[#9](churchyard/numbered-stones.md), "North-West of Church, near West boundary".

### Private Walter George Long (d.1916), aged 40

He was born in Dalston, London, serving with 2nd/6th Battalion East Surrey Regiment, who were training at Gore Street, Monkton. He died of natural causes – heart failure – and as an older man, he was part of the base personnel rather than training for active service. But he wanted to serve his country irrespective. He died on 10th October 1916 - his funeral was at Monkton Church on 14th October 1916, conducted by a Chaplain to the Forces.

**Official record:** [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://www.cwgc.org/find-records/find-war-dead/casualty-details/365927/walter-george-long/) –
East Surrey Regiment, 2nd/6th Bn., service no. 5060. Husband of Florence
Kate Long, of Walthamstow. Buried in grave [#8](churchyard/numbered-stones.md),
which has a Commonwealth War Grave headstone.

## Poems

Poems studied by Monkton School children with their headteacher, Mrs
Janet Graham, as part of their project on the two World Wars (see
“We will remember them”! in
[Special Stories](../history/stuarts-research/special-stories.md)).

### “For the Fallen” by Laurence Binyon (September 1914)

They shall not grow old, as we that are left grow old:\
Age shall not weary them, nor the years condemn.\
At the going down of the sun and in the morning\
We will remember them.

### “In Flanders Fields” by John McCrae (May 1915)

In Flanders fields the poppies blow\
Between the crosses, row on row,\
That mark our place; and in the sky\
The larks, still bravely singing, fly\
Scarce heard amid the guns below.

We are the Dead. Short days ago\
We lived, felt dawn, saw sunset glow,\
Loved and were loved, and now we lie\
In Flanders fields.

Take up our quarrel with the foe:\
To you from failing hands we throw\
The torch: be yours to hold it high.\
If ye break faith with us who die\
We shall not sleep,\
Though poppies grow\
In Flanders fields.

### “The Soldier” by Rupert Brooke (1914)

If I should die, think only this of me:\
That there’s some corner of a foreign field\
That is forever England. There shall be\
In that rich earth a richer dust concealed;\
A dust whom England bore, shaped, made aware,\
Gave, once, her flowers to love, her ways to roam;\
A body of England’s, breathing English air,\
Washed by the rivers, blest by suns of home.

And think, this heart, all evil shed away,\
A pulse in the eternal mind, no less\
Gives somewhere back the thoughts by England given;\
Her sights and sounds; dreams happy as her day;\
And laughter, learnt of friends; and gentleness,\
In hearts at peace, under an English heaven.
