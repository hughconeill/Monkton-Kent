# 26. Farming Fires

After this extremely dry hot summer there were dozens of reports of fires on farms throughout most of England. A lot of these fires happen whilst farmers are harvesting their crops of wheat, barley and oats. Every year in my 30 years of driving combines I heard of at least one machine catching light.

A build-up of dust was the biggest cause for a combine 'going up', especially on top of the engine. From the end of the 1970's, engines became free standing. Direct drive became a thing of the past. All combines became oil driven. The engine's fan was designed to not only cool the engine, but to blow dust away from the engine. All combines now have compressors installed on them to allow the operators to blow off excess dust prior to starting the day's harvesting.

Robert Norrington of Thanet Farm Repairs, our fitter for 20 years or more, once said that someone with 30 years of driving a combine would have had at least one combine 'go up' on him. I had three close calls in my time.

The first was with the CLAAS 108. I overloaded the bin and some grain landed on the manifold or exhaust. I emptied the bin and stopped the combine to have a break. A few minutes into the break I heard a crackling sound from under the combine. I jumped off to see the stubble was alight. I quickly moved the combine out of harm's way. All combines carry fire extinguishers, so I managed to put out the fire before it got out of control.

The second time was when I was driving the CLAAS 470. It was nearly dark and I was cutting the last bin load of the day when I saw David waving and shouting at me to stop. A bearing had collapsed at the rear of the combine and in the half-light he could see sparks flying out from the back of the combine. Fortunately, it did not start a fire.

The third was again with the CLAAS 470. It was the end of the day cutting wheat for Johnathan Tapp. I drove the combine to the far end of the field away from the road, parked and was making my way home when I saw a small fire in the stubble not far away. I managed to put this out. I went back to the combine to see if I could find out what had started the fire, I could see and smell nothing. I rang Robert to tell him what had happened and he said he would be there first thing in the morning. Robert was there before me in the morning and had gone all over the combine and could find no reason for the fire and left. I started harvesting and before I reached the end of the field my computer was telling me that I had a problem and to contact the dealers. I rang them and quoted the code displayed on the screen. They told me I had a problem with my brakes. The brakes are on top of the main axle. A build-up of dust by the pads had caught light and burnt out the wiring to the sensors then fortunately gone out.

I was working with Robert one morning replacing a bearing on the combine, when we saw a pall of black smoke coming from a field not far from us. This was one of the Beaut Farms combines 'going up'. A V-belt by the engine had snapped, wrapped around a hydraulic pipe which broke and sprayed hot oil over the engine. The operator barely had time to grab his lunch bag and get off the machine.

I saw Charlie Quested's combine go up in flames. Charlie farms at West Marsh. I could see where he had parked his combine for the night from our side of the River Stour. He was cutting his field beans and dust had built up somewhere on the machine, caught light and just smouldered overnight. In the morning when the breeze picked up, the dust burst into flames and destroyed the combine.

In the early 1970's, my friend Gordon Morris was doing some field work, driving an old Massey Ferguson 135 next to a field of barley. He turned at the end of the field to see the barley field alight. They think a bit of burning carbon or soot was blown out of the exhaust and was responsible for starting the fire.

The reasons for combines and fields to catch fire is endless, not many are started deliberately.

*Terry Marsh*
