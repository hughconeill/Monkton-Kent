# Final Thoughts

A reminder that I, along with census clerks, and Monkton villagers, try our best to get the facts right, so if there are errors and omissions then please make contact and we can amend for the future.

And “Thank You” to all those people who have contributed information, photographs and articles from their own personal collection, and advice too. Please continue to do so.

I find it fascinating to walk along the “Monkton Centenary Mile of 1926” and imagine the vista of those distant agricultural years. And to learn about the life that villagers lived in those times, even up to the 1970’s.

Careers advice at the end of about 10 years of schooling would come down to farm labourer, cowman, horseman, groom, gardener and shepherd for the majority of boys. And for the girls, paid or unpaid domestic service. There was the opportunity to earn money during the seasons in the orchards, picking hops, potatoes and other crops, and plucking the Christmas turkeys.

I’m surprised at how many people, already living in small 2 up & 2 down cottages, would take in boarders and lodgers – a further source of income – as there were not enough farm tithe houses to go round for all the workers to have accommodation in the village where they worked.

Church attendance was much more time consuming than it is today. Four times every Sunday would be quite a challenge nowadays! I’ve no information on the numbers involved.

It’s admirable that villagers volunteered their time and energies and money for the benefit of all. There must have been fun times in raising money with garden fetes, maypole dancing, bingo nights, duck races and concerts for facilities like the BMX track and the “Sports Field”, the “Village Hall” and the “Men’s Institute”.

The Market Garden small holdings added another level of agricultural success, and the characters who farmed them seemed to have lived long in the memory of villagers today. The likes of Peter Crook, Doug Stannard, Clary Daws, Billie Andrews and Alfred Pink have their place in Monkton’s past glories, providing work for a host of teenagers eager to earn a few shillings after school or at the weekend. And not forgetting the popular and talented blacksmith Fred Harrison, who was a master of his craft, good enough to be a judge at country fairs near and far.

The generosity of the larger farms is also worthy of note – the Fullers, Vinson’s, Baxter’s, Willett’s and Smith’s provided work, opportunities, land and charity to the benefit of the village of which they are an important part.

I started this “Centenary Walk” on noble land at Sheriffs Court Lane – that of Marquis Conyngham. Monkton has also played host to :- …… King Richard 1; Baroness Decies; John Hope de la Poer Horsley Beresford; Jonathan Aitkin M.P.; William Rees-Davies M.P.; Viscount Molesworth; Mrs E.L. Wetherby-Williams and her 2 daughters who ran Monkton’s sophisticated country club during WW2; Sophie, Duchess of Edinburgh; Princess Diana’s personal bodyguard for 6 years, Ken Wharfe, a relative of Alice Dawson, Monkton’s organist/pianist until 1985; Lord Lieutenant of Kent, Allan Willett; Lord & Lady Scarman; Queen Edwina; Robert Dudley, Earl of Essex; Lord Howard of Effingham; …..and Queen Elizabeth II at Buckingham Palace, who used to receive beautiful Monkton cherries each year from Bill NORRIS at “Vinson’s Farm”.

I don’t think that people moved very far away from the village, probably because it would be too expensive or too far to walk to their place of work, and maybe because they could get all they needed to live on from the village itself. Food was available on their doorstep from their own gardens, their neighbours, local market gardens, from the local farms, and from regular deliveries by local tradesmen.

Finally, we all now know about where the blacksmith’s demolished ‘smithy’ was, and that it moved to behind the Drake & Fletcher business opposite the pub; that the Shop had its own bakery; that there was an unofficial sweet shop at Fred Rolfe the cobblers house; where the BMX track was; where 200 Friesian cows were herded down Willetts Hill out to the marshes; that there was a shepherds hut out on the marshes at Docker Hill with a family in permanent residence; that the horses had a right of way through the bar of the pub to get to their stables at the back, and that teachers actually lived in the School !

I hope you consider this a worthwhile exercise, and an interesting way for us to remember times past, to think about how it compares to our lives today, and to pass on to our children, the next generation of ‘Monktonians’! It’s been a lot of fun to compile – perhaps because I always did like history at school. Please keep those pictures and stories coming! Enjoy at leisure!

*Stuart Horsburgh – November 2026.*
