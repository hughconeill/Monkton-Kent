# Bernard Cole’s Interviews

In 1989 and 1991 Bernard COLE – born in 1922 at #4 “Delce Cottages” (see
[Zone 3](centenary-walk/zone-3.md)), a Monkton school governor for 45 years and
a parish councillor for 16 – talked with Monkton villagers, together with
pupils from Monkton School, about their life in the village. Stuart Horsburgh
gathered these recollections, alongside some newspaper profiles of long-lived
villagers, while researching the [Centenary Mile Walk](centenary-walk/index.md).

## Bertha DUNK (b1910)

“I remember Peace Celebrations after WW1, held in a field (Mr FULLER), now part of the “Foxhunter Park”. We had a meal, races, and were given a Peace Mug. The village was a closed community – everyone worked in the village. Once a year, after hop picking, we used to go out of the village to buy clothes for winter. The Vicar of Monkton was Rev. BRACKENBURY. Mr PRIOR was the Headmaster. During the War Mrs BURNHAM was Headmistress. Mr PRIOR came back for a while. Then it was Mrs SIMPSON, Mrs BUDD, Mrs HARRIS. In Infants Class every year some children were away with scarlet fever. School also used to give a medal each year for good conduct and perfect attendance. I won it in 1919, also Charlie ANDREWS – I think we were the last to have them – it was stopped. Mr & Mrs MacMULLAN kept the Village Shop #72, which was a bakery as well. Mr & Mrs KINGHAM kept the “New Inn” pub. Mr BURTON kept the “Forge”. There were trees right through the village, but all gone now, except a few past the Church. Before the “Village Hall” was built, school concerts were held at the “School”. For raising money for the “Village Hall”, Mr MORRIS, who lived at “The Croft” used to make trays, baskets, and rugs to raise money to pay for the Hall. During the school holidays we were able to roam all over the marshes. The Farm only had animals, no machines. All that’s gone now!”

## Elsie FOREMAN nee Barham (b c1920)

“We moved to Monkton in September 1933. I only had 2 terms at “Monkton School” – they were 2 happy terms. When I left school, I stayed at home about a year keeping house, while my mother & father were both working at “Parsonage Farm”. I went out fruit picking etc. Then I went to work at “Monkton Road Farm”, looking after Allan WILLETT (later Lord Lieutenant of Kent). When they evacuated during the Battle of Britain, July-October 1940, I went to work at Baxter’s sausage factory (in Sarre). We worked from 7am to 10pm, 7 days a week. There was a Searchlight Unit at Monkton. Some of my friends were Gladys CROUCHER, Lily HAMMON and Vivian PINK. Her father, Alfred PINK, used to amaze me as he was blind, yet he could go to the pictures and come back and tell you all about it, more than a sighted person could do. He used to sell eggs. Other people I remember are Mr ROLFE who used to mend shoes. Freddie IRELAND lived in the council houses. Frankie GORE lived in the row of houses alongside Mr COLE’s. His father George worked for Mr COLE as a carpenter/coffin maker. The FAGG family lived by the Church. Miss SIMPSON lived at “Millers Lane”. The HOOD family lived at “Monkton Court Drove”. My name was Elsie BARHAM, now Elsie FOREMAN, of 1 Park View, Margate Hill, Acol.”

## George NORMAN (b1906)

“I moved to Monkton in October 1913, aged 7 years. I lived at #10 “Parsonage Cottages” – my mum, dad, 3 brothers and 1 sister. Our neighbours were Mr & Mrs WOODCOCK, and their son & daughters. We went to “Monkton School” : the Headteachers were Mr ROGERS and then Mr PRIOR. Lady teacher was Miss CARTER. We had to be polite and touch our caps if we met our teachers out and call them “Sir or Mam”. The roads were very rough, all stones. It was a horse and trap for transport. Dr. HUNT came on a bike and charged 2/6d (=12.5p) to visit the sick. We used to go on outings to Margate and Ramsgate. Horse drawn carts were called “brakes”. When WW1 broke out, soldiers were stationed at Gore St., and the artillery at “Walters Hall”. They all marched on their way to overseas. We had Peace Celebrations when the War was over. I went to work when I was 14 years old and worked for Mr FULLER at “Sheriffs Court Farm”, from 6am to 5pm, and to 1pm on a Saturday. I have 2 brothers, a sister-in-law, mother and father buried in Monkton Churchyard.”

## Norman HOLDEN (b1915)

“In Monkton Church Choir for 12 years. Rev HENMAN was Vicar and lived with his 2 sisters, both unmarried. Geraldine HENMAN was always visiting sick people and taking them an egg custard. She had a hernia through tugging the church bells. If I go to Heaven, I’m sure she will be there. “Monkton School” children belonged to “WILFRED’S LEAGUE OF GUGNUNCS” sponsored by The Daily Mirror. Fans of “Pip, Sweak & Wilfred” had a coded message : “ICK ICK PAR BOO?” and they replied “GOO GOO PAR NUNK” if they were members. I started work for Mr F. FULLER on 27th December 1928 and worked there until I retired Christmas 1979. My first duty being clearing the farmhouse cellar of water. This I did by standing in a bath and bailing it out. The maids at that time were Phylis ROLFE and Doris REED. My first weekly wage was 11s per week (=55p). Hours were 6.30am till 5pm, and till 1pm on Saturday. After paying mother my keep, my pocket money was 9d (=4p) per week.”

## Ted NORRIS (b c1920)

“NORRIS family lived all their life in Monkton. Married Kathleen NORRIS nee Rayner. Cost of food – 4 and a half old pennies (=2p) for a large loaf; coal 1s 2d (=6p) per cwt. Food was plainer, no baked beans or spaghetti, more dried vegetables (haricot beans and butter beans). Games like climbing, hoops, spinning tops and whips. I worked at “Monkton Court Farm” for my entire working life, except during the years when I was away in the Army. On the farm there were 130 short horn cows; 400 Thanet Down sheep; 24 turkeys in 1934 but 15,000 in 1985. I worked mostly with the cows. When the Doctor visited the house it was 3s 6d (=17p). No milk bottles. Mr WILLETT at “Parsonage Farm” had a milkman with a milk float, and a milk churn, and he would measure the milk into your jug with a pint measure. There were no holidays.”

## Joan HORTON nee Rolfe (b1919)

“Mr ROLFE was a stockman at “Parsonage Farm” for H.T. WILLETT. There’s a picture of him with his champion Friesian bull at the County Show at Margate July 1926.

Mr PRIOR, Headmaster, and Mrs Lilian BUDDS his sister-in-law.

Mr SMITH and his sons lived next to the School in “Waggon Cottage”. The waggon house on a farm often had a separate room, approached by an outside staircase. He had to house the other horsemen of the farm.”

## Mrs BURTON

“Village concerts, held in the School, to raise money for the “Village Hall”. The families used to take extra chairs.

Rev. BRACKENBURY held morning Sunday School in the garden room of the “Vicarage”. Then they would go to morning church. Afternoon Sunday School again in the “Vicarage”. And church in the evening.

Pocket money was 1d a week – a “Saturday Penny”.

Dresses, and pinafores made at school. Also knitted own vests and socks at school.

The road was tarmacked only for the lower end of the village, the rest up to the “Royal Exchange” main road was always stoney.

Children made their own enjoyment and were never ‘bored’.”

## George NORMAN (b1911), speaking in 1991

“He used to help with fruit picking and hop picking after school. He started working at 14 for Mr FULLER at “Sheriffs Court Farm”, and following with Mr FULLER junior at Sheet Farm, Monkton. When he was still at school, during the holidays, he would do bird scaring in the orchards from 5am to 7pm, 7 days per week, for £1.5s a week (=£1.25p).

During WW1 artillery soldiers with their horses and guns were billeted in the big house at “Walters Hall”. I remember the horses tethered in lines on long ropes. Sometimes they would ride through the village. The field from Gore St. Farm to the railway (gas station and animal cemetery etc.) was where the infantry soldiers, the Buffs, were encamped.

The doctor used to come out on his cycle wearing a bowler hat. (Dr HUNT).

The village policeman said they had to behave themselves and if he caught them they would get a hiding. During WW1 and the 1926 Miners Strike there were “Specials” (part-time policemen), village people, like the blacksmith and the shopkeeper etc. There were also several mounted Specials, e.g. Mr COLLARD & Mr H. A. SMITH etc.

He used to push the vicar’s wife to church every Sunday in her wheelchair. He also pumped the organ at church. Some of the children were in the choir.

Mr COLLARD lived at Abbey Farm, Minster and farmed there. He also worked Gore St Farm until Mr WILLETT took it on. Mr COLLARD, who lived at “New House”(“Field House”) was a surveyor.

She remembers when first married her weekly grocery bill for two was 7/6d (=37p).

Mr NORMAN’s father (also George NORMAN) came to Mr H.T.WILLETT’s “Parsonage Farm” as gardener / groom. He used to take care of Mr WILLETT’s horse and his carriage. He would drive them out if they went anywhere. He also did all the gardening and lawn mowing. There was a small electric generator and batteries for the farmhouse and dairy which he had to look after and charge up every day.

“Parsonage Farm” used to grow a lot of potatoes. Used a van with a trough behind the workers. Take the potatoes from the trough into this ?apron? and drop into the furrows. When harvested the potatoes were stored in clamps, covered with 6” straw and 6” earth on top. Then the clamps would be opened in the winter when required and riddled.

Arthur & Cecil BEAN, used to live at the “Royal Exchange” in about 1930 for 9 years. 5 boys & 3 girls. They worked at “Parsonage Farm” for 5 years, then “Monkton Ct Farm” till Arthur retired. Cecil went to St Nicholas Ct Farm, had a serious accident, lost an arm, became a gamekeeper breeding 1500 pheasants, 500 partridges and 300 ducks each year over 3000 acres from Reculver to Minnis Bay to Sarre.

Arthur says Monkton Ct Farm was a real model farm. They’d burn nothing, and everything had a use. Even the bracken would be strimmed, collected and used as a base for winter bedding for bullocks with straw on top.

“Monkton Ct Farm” had a dairy herd of short horns, a flock of Thanet Down sheep, a breed the farm developed. The shepherd was Mr PETLEY.

The waggoner was Mr Eddie FINCH, Mr W ‘Billie’ ANDREWS was the 2nd mate.

Arthur injured his leg whilst working with a threshing machine. When he finished working on the farm he worked the garden for Mr M SMITH. Later he went to St Nicholas Ct Farm to help his brother Cecil with the ‘game’.

Never had wellington gumboots. Hobnail boots with two tongues which made them watertight, and leather leggings which fitted the lower part of the leg and over the boots.

Cecil says:- “When working with the horses at “Parsonage Farm” close to Monkton chalk pit, he related an incident about a circus going from Canterbury to Ramsgate, including the large animals (elephants etc) the owners walked from town to town. He said when the elephants were near Sarre, his horses somehow sensed them approaching and ran back to their stables, they couldn’t control them in any way.

The smell of artificial manure would sometimes frighten the horses.”

## Mr Sidney & Mrs Elsie SMITH (b1917)

“Lived in “Church Row Cottages” for 52 years. Parents Percy & Edith SMITH, lived in #3 “Monkton Ct Cottages” all their married life. Percy was the farm foreman (aka farm bailiff) from WW1 days, when he was in charge of the farm whilst Mr H.A. (‘Bert’) SMITH was in the Army (Mr Michael’s father).

Sidney started work on the farm 1924, long weeks, 10s (=50p) per week.

When the farm used to grow peas for Smedleys Frozen Peas, he used to start at 5am and the peas had to be in the factory at Faversham for processing within 2 hours. He used to drive the pea viner (like a special large combine harvester).

Before the harvester, the corn was cut with a “binder” (this machine cut and tied it into sheaves). These were gathered and stacked, usually round stacks. Sidney used to thatch these stacks to keep the corn dry, until winter when a large steam engine and threshing machine would come and remove the corn from the straw.

The old sweeps (windmill sails) have been put onto Sarre Mill.

When Rev HENHAM married Mr & Mrs Smith, his rubber sole came loose and he caught the corner of her veil and tore it (1939)

Sidney still works for Mr Michael SMITH in his garden. Mrs Elsie SMITH helps in the house with cleaning.

Vicar called in most mornings to the School.

“Parsonage Farm” used to be a general farm sheep, cows, horses. “Parsonage Farm” had black and white Friesian cows – these gave a lot of milk. But the short horn (brown and white) gave less milk but much better quality.

Mrs Kathleen MARSH nee Harris #101 – born next door #103 – “Marsh View Cottages”.

Douglas STANNARD #99. His father Frederick STANNARD lived at “Red Cottage” (now “Barnside”). He worked it as a Market Garden small holding.

Douglas left school and he worked at Jezards bakery for 5 years, Minster – wages 10s a week.

He was in West Africa in WW2 and met Field Marshall Montgomery. He was in the 1st The Royal Dragoons for 9 years.

Rev HENHAM talked to himself as he rode his cycle in the street. The boys used to push Miss HENHAM to church in a bath chair.”

## Mrs Marjorie CULLEY nee Fuller (b1921)

“As children she lived at “Fernlea”.

Her father worked the ground opposite “Marsh View Cottages”, and also the fields behind their houses and the school as a Market Garden. It also had chickens, 2 cows and pigs.

Mother used to make butter and ice cream. Her first job was learning to be a tailoress at Deakins in Ramsgate.

Mr CULLY was in the Hampshire Reg during the war, and was stationed in Monkton, met and married here. Lived at Nethercourt Farm, Ramsgate, but came to their present home and worked at her father’s farm.

Mrs Ivy SOUSBY nee Smith, born 25-Dec-1914, “a Christmas present for her mum”. Lived in Sheriffs Court. Fruit picking and hop picking on the farm during August holidays.

Her father was Percy Edwin “Dido” SMITH”, waggoner on the farm. In his spare time, he was in charge of the Minster Salvation Army Band. He also started a band at Monkton Methodist Chapel.

Mrs Ruth JAMES nee Norris lived at “Saxby Cottage”. Her father Mr NORRIS used to drive a big steam engine, used for driving a threshing machine, before the farmers used combine harvesters. The corn was cut and put into stacks until winter, then when convenient the stacks were opened and the corn was threshed. He used to work for Wingham Engineering Co who had several steam engines and threshing sets.

Next door to “Saxby Cottage” were 2 small timber cottages, now demolished, and a barn, now demolished, with the new “Saxby House” built on the site. Mr Doug STANNARD used to have the barn and ground attached. He used to own a landau, horse carriage, as a village taxi service, to take people to Minster or to the station.

Mr W NORRIS used to work on the “Baxter’s Farm”, and Mr VINSON before them. He was in charge of the fruit orchards, mainly cherry, and the hop garden, until he retired. Cherries were sent each year to the Queen at Buckingham Palace from the farm.”

## Mr Ernest SHONK (b1919)

“Born in Monkton 1919, in “Forge Cottage”. He worked at “Baxter’s Farm” at Sarre, and later for H.T. WILLETT of “Parsonage Farm”. When he retired, he was farm foreman for Mr Peter Smith, Hale Farm, St Nicholas. His father William worked for Mr FULLER at ?Sheet? Farm. The farm buildings used to be opposite “Marsh View Cottages”. The farm used to house pigs and a chicken battery.”

## Mr ‘Ted’ MORRIS & Kathleen MORRIS nee Rayner (b1920)

“Living in “Drove Cottage”. Daughter Evelyn LAWRENCE nee Morris, in charge of Monkton Under 5’s in the Men’s Institute. They worked at “Monkton Ct Farm” firstly with the cows and later just before his retirement with the turkeys. The cowsheds have been demolished, together with a large tithe barn, and the Monkton Manor estate built on the site. Mrs MORRIS was in service. Mr MORRIS looks after Lord Scarman’s garden now (in Monkton Manor).

Mr William George ‘Billie’ ANDREWS, moved to Monkton in 1912. Lived at “Solihull Cottages” and “Marsh View Cottages”. He worked at “Monkton Ct Farm” from 1914-65. Started as a back door boy, and later on with horses when he turned 15, until he retired.

Champion ploughman 1 year at Sarre. 7 prizes for the best working pair of horses. 6 firsts, 6 seconds, 5 thirds, 4 fourths, 1 special prize – all at the Isle of Thanet Ploughing matches held every Oct/Nov. 5 prizes at Marsh ?Howes? Show for Best Decorated Cart & Horse.

His bungalow cost £380 in 1938. His waggoner wage was £1.12s per week (£1.60p).”

## Bert ATTWELL (b1916)

“He moved to Monkton in 1919, lived in “Church Row Cottages”. He worked at “Monkton Ct Farm”, and Hale Farm near Birchington (farmer was Mr G FULLER).

“Walters Hall” was a derelict mansion with its grounds and outbuildings crumbling soon after WW2. It was supposed to be haunted.”

## Doris HARRIS nee Read (b1913), speaking in 1989

“I came to Monkton in 1920 with my family. We lived at #1-2 “Church Cottages”. My father was a milkman for Mr SMITH at “Monkton Ct Farm”. I went to Monkton school with my brother and 2 sisters. Mrs BUDDS was my teacher, Mrs SIMPSON was the headmistress. I was very happy at school and enjoyed all my lessons and the friends I made. My best friend was, and still is, Edie LILLIOTT nee Drake. Ivy STEVENS who lived at the “Royal Exchange” house and now lives in Walmer. Also Phyllis HOWLAND nee Rolfe who I worked with for a year at “Sheriffs Ct Farm”. Mr Brackenbury was the vicar. His wife used to run the G.F.S. (Girls Friendly Society) which I attended regularly at the vicarage. I remember dancing around the maypole at “Monkton Court” and “The Croft” (owned by Mr MORRIS) on special fete days. I also belonged to Monkton Church Choir, until I left to move to Alland Grange. Rev HENHAM and his 2 single sisters came to the “Vicarage” and all were very popular. One Miss HENHAM was disabled and was pulled to church in a carriage by Bert STEVEN. My late brother-in-law, Herbert LAWRENCE, always carried the cross at “Monkton Church”. Since my marriage and the war years I have worked as a dental surgery assistant for 25 years. Unfortunately, my husband Ronald HARRIS passed away in 1986.”

## Eric MILLS, speaking in 1989

“When my father, Herbert MILLS, took over as licensee of the “New Inn” pub in 1951, Fremlins the brewers informed him that “horses had the right of way through the bar to the back of the premises”, where presumably they were stabled. No side entrance existed then, being flanked on one side by “Forge House” and the other by the old “Forge” itself, where the blacksmith Fred HARRISON shod most of the horses of the area.

A regular visitor to the “Forge” was Mike HEWITT who walked his horse from as far as Seven Score Farm, and many other customers also walked their horses considerable distances. Fred was assisted in the “Forge” in those days by Jim APPLETON, not a young man, who walked to work and home again from St Nicholas. I can remember them both with coal fire blazing fitting new strakes or iron rims to cart/wagon wheels, with buckets of water drawn from a pit in the “Forge” to throw over the hot iron to cool down and fit tight to the wooden wheels as they hammered away.

At this time Albert BURTON sold his smithy business to “Drake and Fletcher” and during the building of the new premises opposite, early one Saturday afternoon my father and I standing in the bar watched in amazement as strong winds demolished a wall of the shell of the new building!

In the early ‘50’s, Fremlins made deliveries from Faversham to us with a Foden Steam engine dray. It could only be used on certain routes where water could be drawn for the engine boiler.

Other memories I recall are of Percy JARVIS who was then the Postmaster in the shop next door being robbed one afternoon, and of him shouting out at the thieves after it happened. Whether the culprit was ever caught I cannot recall.

Another regular visitor was the baker, Nelson WHITE, who with his sister, baked the bread in Minster and made his deliveries by a horse pulled van and the van being half size in height. He drove on his rounds sitting on the top outside in all weathers. As soon as Nelson stepped on the van step his horse was away home. At times it amazed me how he ever made it to the seat because unfortunately he was disabled.

In the early days the Caravan Site had not started, but when Harry RADCLIFFE started it, a Mrs FORD whose late husband was the Postmaster General of Peru, was the first person to install a caravan on “Walters Hall Caravan Site”, as it was then known.

Reflecting on “Walters Hall Farm”, the Farm then owned by Robert VINSON employed a number of experienced hands both men and women. Kath HARMES being one lady in particular working equally as well as any man.

Hops were then grown and dried after hand picking by coal fires, the hop drier Walter PRITCHARD used to chat to me some evenings sitting in the oast house while waiting for potatoes to be baked in the hot ashes under the fire – delicious flavour with butter, salt and pepper.

During the time the U.S. Airforce was stationed at Manston one of the officers used to love to visit the “New Inn,” stand his round, and chat with Jack BEAKE, Ted CARTHEW, Bob FITALL, Dickie HULKS, Charlie MARSH, Nelson MORRIS, Bert READ, Fred ROLFE and Percy SMITH, who in his advanced age still rode a bicycle down for his evening pint. All of these had their own special tales to relate and some with their own special seat in the bar, and Bert IRELAND on various occasions with his rendering of “A Farmer’s Boy”. Naturally there are many younger ones, too many to list here, who I remember well, especially in supporting the darts team which in those days formed a side each week in the winter months, at home and away, with the reputation of throwing a good dart arrow.

One of my last fond memories of Monkton is of being Father Christmas to the children at the Church Xmas Bazaar held in the hall. This I performed for 24 years. I remember the first one in ’51 when all were expecting the vicar, Rev GOWER-SMITH, to come in the traditional costume as he usually did, but he then announced Father Christmas himself, and the atmosphere of speculation as to my identity, was really enjoyable. And so, my 8 happy years spent living in the village still continued as I played Father Xmas until 1975 when due to my work I had to retire from seeing the joy and thrill on the faces of the children – some slightly reluctant to face a bearded man.”

## Miss E. GAUNT, teacher, speaking in 1991

“My first visit to Monkton was in June 1954 when my sister and I visited her college friend who was then the headmistress of the village school. During their conversation it transpired that she needed an assistant teacher for the infants. As I had recently acquired a new bungalow in Birchington, it was suggested that I should apply for the post, for I had teaching experience in village schools. The KEC helped by waiving the term’s notice that I should have given to Burham School, with 4 members of staff, and where I was very happy. This meant that I was able to commence working with the children of Monkton at the beginning of the school year in September. I don’t think I ever had more than 25 in the class. As time went on the numbers increased so that we had children coming from Minster and Smugglers Leap. And as I had a double bus journey, I was able to see them safely on and off the buses. We had no hooligans then and the bus drivers complimented us on the children’s behaviour. I had been at the school for several years before Miss FOREMAN retired. She was succeeded by Mrs SMITH who was still there when my time came to do the same. We were all very glad when the school building was improved by the addition of a cloakroom with washing facilities and inside toilets. The majority of the children stayed for school dinners which were much enjoyed. The kitchen staff – Mrs WALDRON and Mrs NICHOLS – did a great job and were always willing to help the children during the dinner hour if necessary. The vicar Rev GOWER-SMITH was always a welcome visitor, especially at Christmas!, and was well known to all for he would often attend assembly on a Friday morning. “

## Michael COLE (b1946), writing in 1989

“Miss FOREMAN was headmistress, and she used to shout a lot. She wrote “R” for right rather than a tick.

1/3rd pint of milk in bottles each day. In winter the milk was too cold, in summer it was like lukewarm butter. The straws were made of waxed paper and the ends always collapsed if they got too wet. It was more fun to blow than to suck the straw.

For a treat we used to get sweets from Mrs ROLFE at Fred ROLFE’s cobbler’s house opposite #4 “Parsonage Cottages”. You went to the back door and knocked and spent ages choosing. My pocket money was 3d a week.

During WW2 old W.S. COLE always went the wrong way round the roundabout at the top of Willetts Hill on his way to Ramsgate as the distance was shorter and it saved petrol.

At 7 years old I was able to ride down Willetts Hill on a homemade boxcar truck. Couldn’t do that now!

We bought a tv for the Queen’s Coronation in June 1953 – one of the first in the village.

We made a tableau of the village and used an old gate post from the marshes. Why did the stocks only have 5 holes in them?”

## Bernard Cole’s rough notes from interviews with villagers in 1991

Rev HENHAM, rode a funny cycle, he was always singing and sometimes came down Willetts Hill with no brakes on, and at the bottom would cross over Monkton Street and hit the bank opposite and land in the field beyond (where Seamark Close is now). School pupils used to do sword dancing at the School with Miss SIMPSON. George ROLFE (Senior) used to repair shoes for the villagers. We used to play on the ‘green’ between “New House” (now “Field House”) and the ‘Sports’ Drove (to the playing fields). Postmaster Mr McMULLEN, was also the baker. Miss HENHAM used to organise Girl Guides and Girls Friendly Society. Parish councillors were Henry Thomas WILLETT, Herbert Allnut SMITH, Mr A.W. MILLER.

Mrs SOUSBY nee Smith born in the cottages at top of Sheriffs Court lane (recently owned by Miriam MARGOLYES of tv fame). Her father Percy Edwin SMITH (known as ‘Dido Smith’) was bandmaster of the Minster Salvation Army Band.

Ernie SHONK (b1919), had 1 sister Edith, lived in #2 “Forge Cottages”. He felt the cane at School across his fingers, fell out of many trees and broke his arm 3 times. Friend was Fred FULLER. Worked for Mr BAXTER at Sarre, and later at “Parsonage Farm” for Mr WILLETT. “Everybody knew everyone else from one end of the village to the other”.

“Parsonage Farm” 1914-50 H.T. WILLETT. Friesian herd of cows, 1,000 sheep. Owned before him by Mr LAKE at Sheriffs Court Farm.

From the “Methodist Chapel” to the “Baxter’s Farm” were trees and bushes all along the road edge. From the “Village Hall” to “New House” (“Field House”) were all trees and bushes along the road edge.

R.S. Vinson’s Farm, now Baxter’s Farm. “Walters Hall Farm” (before VINSON was R.L. HETHERINGTON) – sheep, bullocks, hops.

“Post Office” and shop and bakery. Mr McMULLEN and later Mr SMITH.

Mr NORRIS (senior) – steam engine driver with threshing machine. He used to work on the farm but decided he wanted an easier life so changed to engine driver. But he always worked, driving, with Wingham Engineering at Wingham, and used to cycle every day to his engine, either at Wingham or whichever farm he happened to be working on with it.

Mr NORRIS and his family lived at “Saxby Cottage”. Where the new timbered house called Saxby Cottage is now, used to be a barn, owned by Mr STANNARD for his Market Garden small holding.

“Thatch Cottage” – Mrs PEAKE, a London School mistress. Mr PEAKE works in the House of Commons. Mrs PEAKE’s father, Mr BOWIE, used to work for the Guiness Book of Records.

“Walters Hall”, was a derelict mansion, demolished and now 2 bungalows #92 & #94. It was supposed to be haunted. In 1933 it was used as a depot by Callanders Cables who put up the original pylons carrying the electric cables (approx. 3 years). 1938 it was opened as a Night Club but wasn’t open for long. In 1948 the bungalows were built and the “Caravan Site” opened.

Miss Bertha DUNK (b1910):- Her father was a shepherd, then a cowman at “Parsonage Farm”. Coal was £1.2s/cwt (=£1.10p) from Tilmanstone colliery and delivered before 8am. Her 1st job was housework at Mr Vinson’s. Headmaster Mr PRIOR was very strict. Previous Head was Mr POTTER, and after him Mrs BURNHAM who was quite good. Her father was a shepherd at VINSON’s Farm. He had sheep and bullocks for fattening and a house cow to provide milk for the farm, family and workers. Also pigs.

Food had a lot more flavour, which never returned after the War.

“Forge” and smithy was between the “Post Office” and the “New Inn” pub. Mr VAUGHAN worked the “Forge” and later taken on by brother-in-law.

At Docker Hill, Mr MANSFIELD was a shepherd, and previously Mr CARVELL. The sheep used to graze on the marshes. None of them were ploughed, all grass. “Docker Hill” was a cottage, 2 marshes past the railway line. It has been demolished now, just after the War, and the bricks and tiles were used to build the extension of “Waggon Cottage”.

Mrs HOUSELAND and the children at the time, used to wear pinnies, to keep their frocks clean. They used to make the pinnies at school.

The village postman, Mr SETTERFIELD, used to walk with letters, twice a day, as far as Gore St. Prior to Mr SETTERFIELD, Mr DAVIES used to help out at the “Post Office” and Telephone Exchange, which was also a sweet shop. He also delivered the letters. The telephone exchange was manually operated and if anyone phoned and you were out he would take a message for you and phone you later with it.

Pocket money 2d (=1p) per week (Mrs HORTON) – had to wash up, clean knives and forks. Mrs HOWLAND had to pick the bits of cotton off the coconut matting.

Sunday School outings to Herne Bay.

## From the newspapers

### Richard BAX (b1873)

“A keen gardener who still takes a walk around the village, a 1965 newspaper article featured Richard BAX on his 92nd birthday. Agile Mr Bax, who has been exposed to nature’s elements all his life, through having worked for more than 50 years as a farm labourer, has not had a serious illness in all that time. One of a family of 12, he started work on the land when he was 13, at Monkton Pastures? Farm, and worked for the same employers until retiring 22 years ago aged 70. “I have never known anything else but farmwork. My father was a shepherd on the Minster marshes, and when I was old enough to work, I went to the nearest Farm for a job. There was nothing else to do in those days!”

### Alfie BAX (b1876)

A 1966 Isle of Thanet Gazette article on Monkton wrote :- “Alfie BAX is one of the oldest residents of Monkton at 90 not out, and still going strong. Jovial Mr Bax likes his daily walk around the village and is a familiar figure with most of the residents there. His recipe for good health to a ripe old age? Plenty of fresh air like you get here in Monkton”.

### Richard HULKS

GOLDEN DAY REUNION! – “When Mr & Mrs Richard HULKS of Walters Hall Cottages, Monkton (pictured) celebrate their golden wedding anniversary it will mean a big reunion for the family. Some 70 relatives and friends have been invited to a celebration in the “Village Hall”, and among them will be their 7 children, 14 grandchildren, 2 great grandchildren, and the couple’s 2 original bridesmaids and best man. Mr Hulks, aged 77, one of a family of 14, is the fifth to celebrate a golden wedding anniversary. From his early boyhood, he followed in his father’s footsteps and became a shepherd – a job which he kept until his retirement 12 years ago. Because he only earned 15s (75p) a week on the land when first married, Mrs Hulks also went out to work as a hop-picker and for 48 years she never missed a season. During that time, she also raised 7 children.”
