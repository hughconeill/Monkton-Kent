The land you see around you was owned by Monkton’s principal landowner, the MARQUIS CONYNGHAM, an Irish peerage from 1753, but BARON MINSTER OF MINSTER ABBEY was created in 1821, which gave him an automatic seat in the House of Lords. He is the first, but not the last, person of nobility in the Monkton story!

SHERIFFS COURT, MINSTER - traditionally believed to have taken its name from Reginald de CORNHILL, Sheriff of Kent in the reign of Richard 1 (1189-1199), and who is said to have owned the estate. It was the property of Robert de CORBIE when he died in 1366. Passed to Sir Nicholas WOTTON in 1416. Inherited by Lord Henry STANHOPE. Sold to Henry PARAMOR who was the tenant, then through the family until 1652 and passed to the THATCHER family. After many years they sold to Robert WILKINS, who after many years sold it by 1800 to a Mrs TERRY. Historically held on lease from the Conyngham estate. Frederick William FULLER post 1916 to his death in 1948. Then his son Frederick Arthur FULLER post 1948. And subsequently another Frederick FULLER. Presently approx. 500 acres – and farmed by David John FULLER.

Sheriffs Court Farm was farmed by “the FULLER’s” – starting with Frederick William FULLER (1861 – 1948), who took over the Farm from 1916 until his death in 1948.

Relatives of the FULLER’s from Manston would load a piano onto a trailer and sing Christmas carols at the homes of the friendly, generous farmers in Monkton and Gore Street.

TV star Miriam MARGOLYES O.B.E. , (b1941) a British/Australian controversial comedian and actress, owned a cottage in Sheriffs Ct Lane.

There was a Market Garden small holding business called “Lilliotts” just on the corner of Sheriffs Court Lane.

In the first field ( LEFT ) was a barn known as “The Kennels” where fox hounds would be kept. A ‘well-connected ‘tramp was known to live there before it was demolished.

In 1926, the village postman, ( like Mr SETTERFIELD perhaps? ) would walk the whole village twice daily, with only the cottage names to guide him – no Monkton Street numbers!

```{note}
On this Monkton Trail, look for the authentic farm labourers’ cottages, two up two down, without modern extensions. And realise that between the cottages were hedges, barns and sheds, and crops growing as far as the railway line on the left, and the top road, A299, on the right. In addition, there were just a few more substantial cottages and barns dotted about.
```

## Right: “Walters Hall Farmhouse”

A Grade II listed building in 1972, dating from 1633 – 1799. (One of the 22 Listed Buildings in Monkton!) Early farmers were 1847 George COLLETT; 1851 George COLLETT; 1881 Ambrose COLLARD Jnr. Farmed by Robert S. VINSON (1885 – 1974) until 1951 - arable, hops, fruit, and winter beef cattle and a farm shop. Then Michael BAXTER took over the Farm.

The VINSON’s Farm stretched north, all the way up to the A299 (dual carriageway) and south, down to the railway line.

Robert S VINSON (1885 – 1974 – buried in Monkton Churchyard ) was a great charitable benefactor to the village.

He would give 3d to every school child when they went on a school trip.

When his wife Alice died in 1951, he sold the Farm and moved into the Rev. GOWER-SMITH’s “The Vicarage”, as a lodger, until he died in 1974.

It’s remembered by (Miss DUNK) a shepherd for Vinson’s Farm, he had sheep and bullocks for fattening and a house cow to provide milk for the farm, family and workers. There were also pigs.

William NORRIS worked on Vinson’s Farm, and continued on Baxter’s Farm. He was in charge of the fruit orchards, mainly Morello cherries, strawberries, and the hop garden, until he retired. Cherries were sent each year to the Queen at Buckingham Palace from the Farm. There were several orchards for apples and cherries.

It reached the point when “Walter’s Hall Farm” was the only remaining hop garden in Thanet using the traditional method of picking the crop, but automation had to arrive eventually.

In a 1964 newspaper article in the Thanet Times, the headlines are “Automation comes to hop fields” and “Death of a Working Vacation”. The hop-picking machine of Belgium make was installed the year before. At the same time, old hop-pickers are now dying out and not being replaced. Head hop man William NORRIS says; “Machines are quicker, can pick at the peak of ripeness, and continue in bad weather. So 10 acres of hand-picked hops has now become 25 acres. Tractors and trailers make a constant relay down to the fields, collecting bines and bringing them back to the machines. That’s when each bine is put on one of the 24 carriers on a track which feeds it into the rotary picking fingers. The bine continues out of the machine minus hops and leaves and is automatically dropped into a trailer for burning. In the meantime, the hops and leaves fall onto a nylon belt and are taken to a screener where the leaves are sucked out. This leaves just the hops and some ‘strigs’. The strigs are removed by another process, leaving the hops to fall onto a conveyor belt where 6 women remove any leaves that may have escaped the screener. The hops are then bagged and taken to the nearby oast houses. William says : “The machine is there to stay”.

## Left: The oasts

There were hop fields as far as the railway line. “Walters Hall Farm” (Vinson’s / Baxter’s) worked the last hop-garden in Thanet. Originally dried using a coal fire but then updated in the 1960’s with oil fired heating – hence the specially designed louvre ventilated roof on the kilns. One of their hop bags is on display at the Guiness Factory Museum in Dublin.

Paul RAMMELT (1918 – 2008 ) was the head hop man at Walters Hall Farm.

INTERNET MEMORY from Francis Frith Collection photos. “The Smell of Drying Hops”. I remember standing in the oast house with my neighbour Mr Rammelt and my dad Eddie Styles, smelling the marvellous aroma of the hops as they were being dried. It is a smell that brings back many happy memories and stories about the hop fields.”

Kathleen HARMES (1915 – 1992) worked at the Farm. She remembered that the hops were grown nearby, and dried by coal fires. Hop drier Walter PRITCHARD – he used to bake potatoes in the hot ashes. They had a delicious flavour with butter, salt and pepper.

Doug lived all his life in Monkton, except for his time in the army with the 1st Dragoon Guards (Armoured Cars) in the Middle East. Upon leaving school, he was a baker’s boy at Jezards in Minster, and after Army service he worked with a landscaping gang.
