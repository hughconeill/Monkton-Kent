“Willetts Hill” would not have been the busy road that it is today, with a mini roundabout at the bottom. It was the eastern border of “Parsonage Farm” and no more than a farm track, where the cows would return for milking twice a day. In fact on maps, Millers Lane looks more substantial!

The Seamark Close estate of 37 houses and bungalows was built on arable land in the 1960’s. Its entrance used to be a hedge (that Rev Henham used to cycle into !) and was the site for a spectacular decorative1953 structure for the Queen’s Coronation. It had 2 sturdy upright posts with flags, supported by scaffolding, and it had the words :- “ E.R. - FEAR GOD - HONOUR THE QUEEN “. ]

## Left: “Sports Field” – down the ‘Sports’ Drove

Old parchments were found in a well, which was in the field next to the ‘Sports’ Drove that goes down to the Sports Field. The School headmaster, Mr Sam Sexton, collected them and took charge of them. It’s unknown where they are now.

There are small railway sleepers embedded vertically into the ‘Sports’ Drove’s right bank. And a natural spring somewhere I believe.

There are several Memorial trees planted in the ‘80’s and ‘90’s in memory of the FULLER’S Farm family who donated the land to the village, and also a big chestnut tree Memorial to Edith ‘Nellie’ WRAIGHT for her contribution in establishing the Monkton W.I. at the Village Hall.

It’s also been mentioned that a “Doomsday Tree” from 1086 – at the rear of “New House” (“Field House”). But further research says : “there’s no famous Doomsday tree in the Isle of Thanet. There is a notable historical Doomsday Oak in Kent, but it was located at the Godinton House estate near Ashford. That particular tree, said to have been planted around the time of the Norman Conquest (1066) – famously collapsed on 3rd September 1939, the day World War 2 was declared!!! Another Monkton mystery!

Originally the land was farmed by the “FULLER’s Farm”, growing tomato crops.

The Parish Council own the Field. And they would mark out an athletics track for the Monkton School Sports Day.

Monkton Cricket Club were based at the Sports Field, with founding players such as John SHEPHERD, Vic COOK, Ken BURGESS and Charlie WOODCOCK.

MONKTON SPORTS CLUB. 1988 President: Major HEDLEY (“Field House”). Chairman: John BARLOW (“the White Stag pub”). Vice-Presidents: Rev GOWER-SMITH (“the Vicarage”), M SMITH J.P. (Monkton Court Farm”), Mr C. BEECH. Hon.Sec.: C.J. WOODCOCK (“Brabourne”).

It was a successful effort by worthy local volunteers, to get the field grassed (Ted and Gordon MORRIS ploughed and grassed the field) and the Pavilion built c1980’s. (John POTTER and Dave NORTH playing a major part). There was Charlie WOODCOCK who was at the forefront of it, and there used to be a plaque to his significant contribution. Now he has a bench in the corner of the field next to the Beacon built by Bill Hayward [Rachel Horsburgh’s father from Herne Bay] for the1994 Centenary Celebrations. To raise funds for the building, there were Duck Races at Plucks Gutter, Meat Raffles were held in the White Stag pub, and regular Wednesday night Bingo evenings in the Village Hall that got the village involved, and the “Useless Eustace” fund-raiser. (Research says Eustace is “a hotch-potch of the many asses who cheerfully go through life doing the wrong thing at the wrong time” – a cartoon character in the Daily Mirror from 1935. But I don’t know how it was used to create a fund-raising activity?) Peter ATTWELL and Ken BURGESS were among the helpful volunteers. (***APOLOGIES to any others who contributed but are not named here!***)

The Monkton Football Club for young and old was started by parish councillor John WAY, and weekend football was in full flow by the 1990’s and continues to this day.

The BMX track in the 1980’s was the work of Seamark Close resident Don GREGG and village volunteers, and local businessman Les REAY. The track was on the far right-hand side of the Sports Field, and created with mounds of earth moulded into an up and down shape - similar to a modern day skate board park - mainly for youngsters in the village, but also used by enthusiasts from other nearby towns. The Monkton team were called “The Mad Monks” and another Seamark Close resident Tony TURNER was the official race starter.

On VE Day in 1945 Bob HOOD had to plough all the cucumbers into the ground because the Dutch could once again flood the marketplace after the War and undercut UK prices.

The initial plan for a Sports Field was to put it on the land next to Major HEDLEY’s “New House” (“Field House”) and the 2 attached cottages. But objections to it led to deciding to put it where it is now – on FULLER’s farmland where there were cauliflowers and cucumbers. In 1978 the Parish Council, chairman Julie COOK, Michael SMITH, Bernard COLE, Charlie WOODCOCK and the village volunteers got things under way, with Mary TYRELL giving permission to use the ‘Sports’ Drove for access.

Trevor WILLETT was one of 3 farming brothers – he was based at Parsonage Farm in Monkton. Harry lived and worked at Gore Street Farm. And Charlie lived and worked the Farm in Acol.

In a 1966 newspaper article, Trevor WILLETT is pictured, and notes there are 400 cattle, pedigree Friesians traceable to the first ones introduced into this country. It had black and white Friesian cows which gave a lot of milk, although the brown and white short horn gave less milk but much better quality. The bull pen which overlooked Willetts Hill was always a popular sight for locals, but it was only a wooden fence that kept it out of reach, and could have perhaps been stronger? As was the daily ‘drove’ of the cows to and from the marshes along the ‘Sports’ Drove to the milking sheds in the farmyard – it must have been quite a sight to see! The Farm had its own milkman with a horse drawn milk float and a milk churn, and he would measure the milk into your jug with a pint measure. No milk bottles then, or plastic! There were also 1,000 sheep, horses, corn, and a lot of potatoes on his 1,200 acre farm. Trevor’s owned it since 1950, taking over from his father, Henry T Willett (1866 – 1945). Trevor was a keen horseman and at one time in the 1960’s rode with a pack of hounds that were kennelled halfway down the ‘Sports’ Drove.

In an early map of 1769, “Parsonage Farm” was known formerly as “Amboury Farm”.

Henry COLLARD was a surveyor, who once lived at “Abbey Farm” in Minster and he farmed there. He also worked the “Gore Street Farm” until Thomas Matthew WILLETT bought it.

Major HEDLEY (1908-1987) was a well-known character in the village, infamous for his dangerous driving skills. His family had a very early interest in the 1813 “Hedley’s Puffing Billy” steam engine. And later they had a family soap business in Newcastle that was the originator of the Fairy Liquid brand. It was bought by Proctor & Gamble in 1930.

The house had extensive grounds, and villagers would play on the green between the house and the ‘Sports’ Drove. On the other side of the house was an orchard that was once mooted as the site for a village Sports Field.

Major HEDLEY was a generous benefactor in the village and gave every Monkton School pupil 6d (2.5p now but worth a lot more then!) when they went on a school trip.

In the past, the house belonged to the church, and it is also linked to “Parsonage Farm”.

Mrs BRACKENBURY used to run the Girls Friendly Society at “The Vicarage”.

Rev BRACKENBURY (vicar from 1915-27) held Sunday School in the garden room of “The Vicarage”. Then they’d all go to morning church. In the afternoon there was Sunday School in the Vicarage. And church in the evening!

Rev HENHAM (Monkton vicar from 1927-47) rode a funny bicycle, talked to himself as he rode along, and sped down Willetts Hill with no brakes, going straight across the road and into a hedge/bank (where Seamark Close is now).

Rev HENHAM had two unmarried sisters, Geraldine and Eveline. Geraldine would always visit the sick with an egg custard. Miss Henham would organise the Girl Guides, and the Girls Friendly Society.

Bert STEVENS, and George NORMAN, were two of the boys who would push Miss HENHAM to church every Sunday in her bath chair because she was disabled.

Doris HARRIS nee Read in 1989 remembers….. dancing around the maypole at #“Monkton Ct” and #“The Croft” on special church fete days.

Rev GOWER-SMITH, (Monkton vicar from 1947 – 1974), was usually Father Christmas for the Church Xmas Bazaar in the “Village Hall” but surprised everyone one year when Father Christmas appeared in his presence. It was the “White Stag” publican, Herbert MILLS, and Herbert played the role for 24 years, from 1951 – 1975.

Rev GOWER-SMITH was the last vicar to live at “The Vicarage”, (he died in 1984). Afterwards it became a Residential Home, “Gower House”, but closed down in the 1990’s. It’s now a private home.

## Right: “Royal Exchange”

*A former coaching Inn and pub – Grade II listed since 1987.*

Monkton was in a prime spot for pilgrimages from Canterbury to Minster to visit the burial place of the much-revered Saint Mildred of Thanet. Joined by other visitors stepping off the ferry at Sarre – when the sea came up that far! Thus, the main road through Monkton has witnessed a great deal of activity over the years.

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, Monkton was on the main turnpike route to Ramsgate, and horse drawn coaches would stop at the “Royal Exchange” before proceeding along Millers Lane to the Prospect Inn at Mount Pleasant (Minster roundabout). But with the advent of the railway from the 1850’s onwards, the Inn’s staging function became redundant, and it closed as an Inn in 1893.

In 1770 it was the haunt of John Gambrill and his gang. They had a fight with the police outside the pub and were put in the nearby stocks. When they were released, they captured the constable’s daughter and put her in the stocks. They were sent to Maidstone gaol for a year. And surprisingly, upon his release, John Gambrill later married the constable’s daughter! It was designated a Grade II Listed building from 10th April 1987.

## Left: “Monkton Court Farm”

*Grade II listed in 1986.*

According to Ted NORRIS in 1991, who worked his whole life at the Farm, the Farm had 130 short horn pedigree cows, 400 Thanet Down/Romney Marsh sheep, 24 white turkeys in 1934 (but 15,000 in 1985!) He worked mostly with the cows (except during WW2).

It was a model farm – and nothing was wasted. Dairy herd of short horns, a flock of Thanet Down sheep, a breed that the Farm developed. Shepherd was Mr PETLEY. Waggoner was Mr Eddie FINCH, Mr W. ‘Billie’ ANDREWS was the 2nd mate.

The Farm used to grow peas for “Smedleys Frozen Peas”. It meant a 5am start because the crop had to be at the factory in Faversham for processing within 30 minutes (2 hours was also mentioned?)

The cowsheds and a large tithe barn were demolished and replaced by the present Monkton Manor estate.

Marjorie ‘Mo’ SMITH initiated the introduction of turkeys to the Farm, having researched the subject in America. Violet May HAMMON was a key figure in the success of the turkeys at “Monkton Court Farm”. The HAMMON’s and the JOHNSON’s are also remembered as being part of the operations success.

The turkey sheds were down “The Drove”, beyond the 2 cottages, and were old Manston Army sheds. One was an American bowling alley. Other sheds for various mechanised operations were between there and the Farmhouse. Quite an industrial operation year-round, but especially at Christmas.

Monkton Ct Farm changed a great deal with the loss of the various animal stocks i.e. the milking herd, the sheep and finally the white turkey flock. But in contrast it was the last farm to have a working horse, cared for by William ‘Billie’ ANDREWS, and able to work a horse-drawn plough.

William “Billie” ANDREWS (1908-1996) worked at “Monkton Ct Farm” from 1914 – 65. Started as a back door boy, then worked with the horses. He was a champion ploughman with prizes at ploughing competitions and shows. “Blossom” was his horse., and poignantly Blossom led his master’s coffin up to Monkton Church and patiently stood by the grave for the burial.

Monkton Ct Farmhouse used to be the Manor House. In 961 Queen Edwina gave the land/manor to the monks of Christchurch Canterbury. The village was known as “MONOCSTUNE” in the Doomsday Book 1086.

In 1480 a new dormitory for the monks was built by Prior Selling, possibly the precursor to the present Monkton Court. Remained in the possession of the monks until the Dissolution by Henry 8th in c1538, and surrendered to the Crown, to Elizabeth 1, to her favourite Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, then back to the Cathedral. When the north aisle of Monkton Church was removed, an immense quantity of masonry, bits of the moulding of arches, pillars and capitals, were reused for the Farmhouse garden.F

Sidney “Snowy” SMITH – his father George SMITH was bailiff at Monkton Ct Farm for over 60 years. George & Snowy started at Monkton Court Farm from the age of 14. George worked there for 56 years. Snowy was the Farmhouse gardener, and his wife Elsie worked in the Farmhouse. They lived in #5 “Church Row Cottages”. Snowy was still working part-time (in 1990?) after 58 years.

April 1970 was the “Big Farm Blaze”, as headlined in the east Kent Times newspaper. “Farmworkers and villagers save £10,000 herd”. A photo shows the heroes of the rescue leaning on a gate – Jack POTTER, Eileen POTTER, Tom HULKS, Phil SMITH, Ray CHRISTIAN, George SMITH, Ted MORRIS and Alf MORRIS (not pictured).

As asbestos roofing exploded and blazing bales of straw and hay cascaded into the pens full of terror-stricken animals, Mrs Potter and the other fire heroes fought to free the trapped animals. 2 cows died, 1 had to be put down, and 5 were injured but recovered – out of 90 cows!”. Stocks have stood in front of the Church since the C15th. They are a feet restraining device used as a form of corporal punishment and public humiliation. Victims may be insulted, kicked, tickled, spat on, or subjected to other inhumane acts – including traditionally the throwing of rotten tomatoes! England’s “Statute of Labourers 1351” prescribed the use of the stocks for ‘unruly artisans’ and required that every town and village erect a set of stocks. The 1351 Act was repealed in 1863, but it has never formally been abolished!

A Grade II Listed structure by English Heritage from 1963. It’s not thought that the present stocks have any antiquity. They have been sold and replaced many times over the years, sometimes even sold to American military based at Manston – a memento of “ye olde merrie England!”!

In the Parish Council 1994 Centenary Celebration booklet, they were about to be repaired with antique matching timber that had to be tied down in the river for a month in order to weather it! They suggested that when completed it was brought back into use i.e. if any vandals are caught in the act of their pranks, that they be given the opportunity of spending a period in the stocks with its consequences and no criminal record – or to be handed to the police and gain a criminal record! It’s lucky for us that we don’t have a ducking stool ! Or even a gallows !!!

It’s known that c1770, local gang leader John GAMBRILL was put in the Stocks for fighting the police outside the “Royal Exchange” pub.

Interestingly one old photo shows two stocks together, the second one having a cover above it !

In 1988, Monkton took 2nd place in a county-wide competition organised by The Men of Trees. The prize was a free tree and 5 shrubs donated by the Kent Messenger Group – publishers of the Thanet Extra – who sponsored the contest. A spokesman for Monkton Parish Council said it was intended to plant a Golden Hornet on the triangle of land by the village stocks. Now you know!

St Mary Magdalene is the smallest of Thanet’s seven ancient churches, and although mentioned in the Doomsday Book of 1086, the present church is basically Norman, dating from around 1190. It has a simple design of chancel, nave, porch, tower and now with a peal of 6 bells. The churchyard is the last in Thanet open for church burials and is looked after by volunteers. In 1963 it was designated as a Grade I listed building – the highest level of historical antiquity.

In a 1910 picture of the Church there is a flagpole structure on top. I wonder how many years that was in place?

Alice Dawson (d.1985) was the organist for many years. In the days when the old organ was pump-powered by volunteers, boys could earn 6d on a Sunday for pumping. After the organ was electrified, there was a power cut, with a christening imminent, and Terry MARSH was contacted as he was the only person who could remember how the original pump worked.

In 1983, I (Stuart Horsburgh) was a new villager (from Seamark Close) and I attended the Sunday morning church service taken by Rev Houldsworth. It was short, with only one hymn sung unaccompanied. After a few weeks I told the vicar I could play for hymns if he wished, and for the past 43 years I’ve been the resident organist. I played the small, C19th original pipe organ until its demise in 2003, and since then have played a Viscount Jubilate 235 2 manual electronic organ with a full set of pedals – and no pipes!

Around 1984, in an “Adscene” newspaper article, “Blessed bureaucracy! Red tape puts a stop to plan for restoration of church”, Restoration Appeal Fund secretary Tony TURNER (Seamark Close) said that the award of grants is being delayed by red tape. The village has raised £10,000, but over £20,000 is needed, and inflation is rising all the time. The village had previously fought a diocese decision to close the church and make it redundant. “The congregation felt that over the last 800 years the small population of Monkton have maintained the church and because of this fought to save it”.

And then around 1988 (after the Great Storm of 1987) there was a big Appeal to restore the church – raising £60,000 from the village and various grants and benefactors. A newspaper article quotes churchwarden Tony TURNER (Seamark Close); “It was a significant effort by people over a long time. With a third of that figure donated by the villagers themselves, there was strong community spirit and a feeling of St Mary’s holding a special place in their hearts. Almost the whole village believed that this lovely old church was worth saving, not necessarily for the present congregation but for generations to come.” The current work involves replacing defective tiles and timber and re-covering the tower roof. But a further £60,000 for the next phase to repair the external stonework and internal plastering is another challenge to be faced in the near future”.

An early map of 1769 shows buildings around the Church, including a small candle works.

The ancient board of past Monkton clergy, 1291 – 1996, which is seen in the porchway, was written in old fashioned copperplate calligraphy by Seamark Close resident and village volunteer Don GREGG, during the 1990’s.
